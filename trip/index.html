DAYS 1–2 — SUN SEP 6 & MON SEP 7

Departure & Helsinki

Long haul Sunday, then landed Monday to Café Engel across from the cathedral, the market square, a ferry out to the fortress, and a walk back to Löyly for saunas and Baltic plunges — a highlight of the trip.

What We Did

  • Lunch at Café Engel, Senate Square. C. L. Engel’s neoclassical square, laid out after Helsinki became the Grand Duchy’s capital in 1812; the cathedral was St Nicholas’ Church until Finland’s 1917 independence.
  • Kauppatori, the harbor market, on the walk to the ferry — reindeer sausage, salmon on dark rye, Karelian pies and cinnamon korvapuusti.
  • Suomenlinna, on foot. Built from 1748 as the Swedish fortress Sveaborg; surrendered to Russia in 1808 with barely a fight, still argued over today. UNESCO World Heritage since 1991, home to about 800 people. Walked the spine to the King’s Gate and back.

Kuopio: The Smoke Sauna

North into the Lakeland on Kallavesi, straight to Jätkänkämppä, the world’s largest smoke sauna, that night.

Logistics

  • Two nights at Spa Hotel Rauhalahti, lakeside on Kallavesi, up by train from Helsinki.

What We Did

  • Jätkänkämppä, Tuesday evening. The world’s largest smoke sauna — 108 bathers at once, at record — in a 1950s lumberjack lodge 600 m from the hotel. No chimney: the fire burns all day and the smoke clears before anyone bathes in the soot-black room. Into the sauna first, walking straight down into Kallavesi whenever the heat got too much, then the buffet dinner with accordion music.

DAY 4 — WED SEP 9

Kuopio: Museum, Market & Puijo

A museum in the morning, the market hall for lunch, a misty ridge trail in the afternoon, and dinner turning slowly above the forest as the sun went down.

What We Did

  • Kuopio Museum, Wednesday morning. Natural and cultural history share an Art Nouveau building from 1907, Finland’s third purpose-built museum. Walked through a reconstructed savupirtti, a chimneyless cabin like Jätkänkämppä’s sauna the night before.
  • Kuopio Market Hall. Home of kalakukko, the Savo classic — whole vendace baked shut inside a rye loaf, eaten like a pie.
  • Labyrinth with Ellis, waiting for the skies to clear. Not pictured: Greg asleep in the chair next to us.
  • Puijo ridge trail, Wednesday afternoon. On foot through the spruce before the tower, mist still low from the morning’s rain. Puijo rises about 150 m over Kallavesi, a protected nature reserve since 1928.
  • Puijo Tower, Wednesday evening. Dinner at Scandinavia’s first revolving restaurant, opened 1963 atop the 75-meter tower, turning slowly over Kallavesi as the sun went down. Kuopio sits in Savonia, the region the Forest Finns left for Värmland and Finnskogen — whose trail the trip picks up later.

DAY 5 — THU SEP 10

Turku: Cathedral & Library

In by train from Kuopio, then Ellis took over as our map navigator, leading the walk from the cathedral to dinner.

Logistics

  • In by train from Kuopio via Tampere — IC 142 then IC 916, into Turku City Centre (Logomo) at 13:50. One night at Hotelli Helmi, eight minutes from the castle.

What We Did

  • Turku Cathedral, Thursday afternoon. First stop on the walk Ellis navigated end to end. Finland’s “Mother Church,” consecrated in 1300 and still the most important building in the country’s Evangelical Lutheran Church. The 1827 Great Fire gutted the tower and nave; Carl Ludvig Engel — the same architect behind Helsinki’s Senate Square — rebuilt the altar, pulpit and tower in the 1830s, so most of the interior seen today postdates the fire. Queen Karin Månsdotter, the only Swedish-era royal buried in Finland, rests in a black marble sarcophagus in a side chapel.
  • Sibelius Museum. Åbo Akademi’s music museum, in a 1968 Brutalist building by Woldemar Baeckman with a hyperbolic-paraboloid concrete shell inside. The Sibelius name is honorary, not biographical — the composer was from Hämeenlinna and never lived in Turku, but gave the museum his blessing to use his name in 1948. Roughly 2,300 instruments in the collection, some of them open for visitors to actually play.
  • Turku Main Library. The 1903 building Ellis led us to, BIBLIOTHECA carved over the door and the bronze Petrelius lion fountain out front at Vähätori — one of Finland’s oldest public library buildings, third after Helsinki and Rauma. A glass extension by JKMM Architects was grafted on in 2007, doubling the space and linking old to new by a walkway.
  • Dinner at Ravintola Rioni. Georgian home cooking — khachapuri, cheese-filled bread, and khinkali, soup dumplings — a hard turn from a week of Finnish tables.

DAY 6 — FRI SEP 11

Turku: Castle & the Night Ferry

E-bikes out to Ruissalo and back, a swing through the castle museum, one more pass through the handicrafts quarter, then the night ferry out.

Logistics

  • Friday evening — Viking Grace sailed at 20:55 from Turku, overnight to Stockholm. Cabin Seaside Four (S4), sea-view porthole.

What We Did

  • E-bikes, Friday morning. Rented electric bikes in town and set out along the Aura riverbank, aiming west for Ruissalo — the easiest way to cover an island that’s mostly forest and shoreline road. Twenty miles by the time we got back.
  • Turku Castle. Finland’s largest surviving medieval castle, its round keep begun around 1280 at the mouth of the Aura to guard the river approach, with a lower Renaissance bailey added around the courtyard in the 1500s. Duke John and Catherine Jagiellon held their court here before he became king, and Queen Karin Månsdotter — the same queen buried in the cathedral — spent four years under house arrest in its rooms in the 1570s. A 1614 fire and heavy WWII bombing both did real damage; architect Erik Bryggman led the long restoration that reopened it as a museum in 1993, the medieval keep and whitewashed Renaissance range now walked as one building.
  • Out on Ruissalo. An island of oak forest and 19th-century summer villas, annexed into Turku in 1845 and still laced with the botanical garden and the old villa lanes along the water — the kind of place built for exactly this, riding past one property line after another with the Baltic on one side.
  • Kuuvanokka’s 1843 rock carving. On the cliffs out past the villas, a date — 1843 — cut into the bare rock at Kuuvanokka, rediscovered only within about the last decade. No one knows who carved it or why; it’s one of those small unexplained things a place like Ruissalo turns up if you’re out far enough to find it.
  • Back to Luostarinmäki. A second pass through the handicrafts museum, this time through the textile workshop for a closer look at the upright loom — one of the trades the museum keeps alive by leaving the tools exactly as the last craftsman set them down.
  • Turku Market Hall. A last walk through before the ferry — Finland’s oldest covered market, trading since the 1890s under one long Gustaf Nyström-designed roof, stalls still doing the same business they always have.
  • Forum Marinum. Finland’s national maritime museum, right on the Aura by the castle — an indoor hall of model ships and shipbuilding exhibits, plus a fleet of real museum vessels moored outside: the full-rigged sail training ship Suomen Joutsen, the barque Sigyn, and the minelayer Keihässalmi, a Cold War–era ship (1957), not the WWII vessel it’s sometimes billed as. Close enough to the ferry terminal to make it the last stop before boarding.

DAY 7 — SATURDAY SEP 12Current Day

Stockholm

Off the overnight ferry at dawn, then Skansen, the Vasa, and meatballs in a 1733 beer hall.

Logistics

  • Sleeping at Hôtel Reisen in Gamla Stan, 2 rooms. Built in 1617, on the waterfront, steps from the Royal Palace — the spa is carved into 17th-century brick vaults.

What We Did

  • Off the ferry at dawn. Docked in Stockholm and into Gamla Stan, the medieval old town four blocks wide — narrow enough that some of its alleys run under four feet across.
  • Skansen. The first open-air museum anywhere, opened by Artur Hazelius on 11 October 1891 and copied by nearly every open-air museum in Scandinavia since. Around 150 buildings were moved here from across Sweden, almost all of them original rather than replicas. We went through the Nordic animal exhibits and walked the old farmhouses and town buildings — a full working sample of Swedish life before it started disappearing.
  • Past the Nordiska Museet. The grand Nordic Renaissance–revival building next to Skansen — Hazelius’s other museum, founded in 1873 and housed here since 1907, built to hold everyday Swedish cultural history rather than royalty or war.
  • The Vasa. A 226-foot warship that sank twenty minutes into its maiden voyage in 1628 and was raised almost intact in 1961, still about 95% original timber. Six levels of walkway circle it in the dark.
  • Challenging the Deep. A temporary exhibit inside the Vasa Museum on James Cameron’s 2012 solo dive to the Challenge Deep — the deepest point of the ocean, nearly 36,000 feet down — built around a replica of his submersible, DEEPSEA CHALLENGER.
  • Dinner at Restaurang Pelikan. A Södermalm beer hall trading since 1733, Art Nouveau fittings intact. The meatballs came the orthodox way — cream sauce, lingonberries, pickled cucumber, pressed potato — alongside a roast, mashed rutabaga, potato purée and a trio of mustards, shared around the table.

DAYS 8–9 — SUN SEP 13 & MON SEP 14

Finnskogen

Into the Forest Finn heartland. Two nights up in the pines in a glass-walled cabin.

Inland: cooler nights, low 40s possible Peak autumn color
Ancestry: Kapistorp — where Elias Lund was born
Hertz rental carPAN Tretopphytter — Pan 2Rossum visit — option 1, Mon

Logistics

  • Sun 8:00 AM — Hertz pickup in Stockholm, then about 6.5 hours west, crossing into Norway.
  • Mon — a full day in Finnskogen, with the car parked unless it is wanted.
  • Sleep both nights: PAN Tretopphytter, cabin Pan 2. Architect-designed A-frames on steel stilts, well up in the pines — spiral staircase, canopy deck, floor-to-ceiling glass, full kitchen, wood stove.
  • The cabins are self-catering — there is no restaurant at PAN, and the last groceries are in the town before the turn-off.
  • Getting around: the rental car. Nothing out here runs on public transit, and cell coverage is patchy.

Must-Do

  • Kapistorp, on the way in. This is Greg’s route and Greg’s reason. The road from Torsby up toward the border runs through the country where the family’s Finnish side spent most of their time in Sweden, and Kapistorp is where Elias Lund, Elliot’s great-great-grandfather, was born. It sits on the line between Stockholm and the cabins — a place to stop the car, get out, and look at what they were working.
  • One forest, two countries. Kapistorp sits on the Swedish side and PAN on the Norwegian, but until the border was fixed in 1751 that was one Forest Finn community moving freely through the same woods. Forty minutes of driving separates them now. It took a treaty to put the line there.
  • Why any of this is here. The Forest Finns came out of Savonia and northern Häme between the late 1500s and the mid 1600s, reaching this side of the border from the 1620s; a census in 1686 counted 1,225 of them in Norway. They lived by svedjebruk — felling and burning standing spruce, then sowing a rye bred to germinate in the ash — which let them farm standing forest that no plough could touch, and is exactly why the family settled deep in the trees. The border drawn in 1751 cut one community in two.
  • An evening in the cabin: cook, light the wood stove, sit out on the canopy deck. No traffic and no light pollution.

If Time Allows

  • Finnetunet at Svullrya, 46 minutes east. The outdoor farmstead is open all year and free to walk — thirteen buildings brought in from Grue and Brandval Finnskog, including all three Forest Finn smoke-heated types together, which is very nearly unique. On a Monday it is open to wander: the exteriors, the way the yard is laid out, and the forest around it, with an hour and a half of driving to get there and back. Worth it for the place itself.
  • The Rossum visit, if Monday is the day it comes together — a distant relative the family is hoping to meet. Still being arranged; the fallback is Tuesday around the drive into Oslo.
  • Old settler trails through birch, pine and spruce on paths cut four hundred years ago.
  • Foraging: mid-September is peak lingonberry and mushroom season and the forest floor is covered.
  • Torsby Finnkulturcentrum sits right on Sunday’s route, in the town on the road to Kapistorp, and it is the museum for exactly this country. Its published season runs to 31 August, so ring ahead for September hours. If it is open, it is the obvious place to ask about the Lunds.

Down from the forest into the city. A stave church on Bygdøy, the sculpture park, and a table in a 17th-century cellar.

Oslo: mid-Sep avg highs near 57°F Autumn foliage underway
Rossum visit — option 2 (family)
Comfort Hotel Xpress YoungstorgetStatholderens — 6:30 PM

Logistics

  • Tue morning — ~2-hour drive down from PAN, in Oslo around midday.
  • Rossum visit — second window. The distant relative the family is hoping to connect with. If it didn’t happen Monday, today is the day. With dinner fixed at 6:30 it wants to sit in the afternoon, either side of the drive down — the last thing still floating on the whole trip.
  • Check in at Comfort Hotel Xpress Youngstorget, family-sized room — home base for the rest of the trip. Greg keeps it every night through Friday morning; Ellis and Elliot are away Wednesday.
  • Tue 6:30 PM — Statholderens, ten minutes on foot from the hotel.
  • Getting around: Ruter transit pass. Central Oslo walks well, and the tram covers the rest.

Must-Do

  • Norsk Folkemuseum on Bygdøy (daily 10–17 through September; bus 30, or the ferry from Rådhusbrygge). Founded in 1894 by Hans Aall, who then ran it for 52 years, and in 1907 it swallowed King Oscar II’s collection of 1881 — generally counted the world’s first open-air museum. That is how the Gol Stave Church came to be here: built around 1200 in Hallingdal, bought when the parish wanted a new one, given to the king and re-erected at Bygdøy in 1884. Precision is worth having here: the west portal carvings are original, the south portal is an 1884 copy, and much of the outside was reconstructed on Borgund’s model — but the 1652 chancel paintings are the real thing. Around it are complete farmsteads moved in from Setesdal and Numedal.
  • Vigeland Sculpture Park: 200-odd bronze and granite figures over 80 acres, free and open at any hour. The Monolith is 46 feet of intertwined bodies cut from one granite block over fourteen years.
  • Dinner at Statholderens Mat & Vinkjeller, 6:30 PM. The casual cellar room under Statholdergaarden, working off the same kitchen as the Michelin floor above, in 17th-century vaults ten minutes on foot from the hotel. Booked, three places, à la carte.

If Time Allows

  • Historical Museum on Karl Johans gate — the swap if Bygdøy is a bridge too far, and only ten minutes from the hotel. Its VIKINGR exhibition has the runestones, weapons, silver hoards and the Oseberg gold and textiles.
  • Oslo Opera House: walk up the angled marble roof to the harbour. Free, five minutes from the hotel.
  • Akershus Fortress: a 700-year-old castle on the harbour with free guided tours.
  • National Museum of Norway: opened 2022, five minutes from Vigeland, with Munch’s The Scream.

DAY 11 — WEDNESDAY SEP 16

West to Sogndal

Across the mountains to the Sognefjord. Eight hundred years of timber on the way.

Mountain passes: possible early snow Sunset around 7:45 PM
Ellis + Elliot west — Greg on the Oslofjord
VRBO cabin, SogndalGreg — Oslo tour, 10 AMComfort Hotel — Greg

Logistics

  • Wed, early — out of Oslo. Roughly 7 hours of driving, stops included.
  • West on the E16 through Valdres and over the mountains toward Lærdal.
  • Midday — Borgund Stave Church, right off the E16. Budget an hour.
  • Late afternoon — on to Sogndal, then up to the cabin.
  • Meanwhile in Oslo — Greg’s combo tour leaves at 10:00: the Grand City Tour by coach, then a cruise out into the Oslofjord. Conf 1441106847.

Must-Do

  • Borgund Stave Church (open to 14 October, 09:30–17:00, twenty people inside at a time). Built in 1180 and dedicated to St Andrew, the best-preserved stave church in Norway and almost entirely original timber — seven tiers of roof, dragon heads along the ridges, and beside it the only free-standing medieval stave bell tower left in the country.
  • Vindhellavegen — 1.7 km and about an hour, straight from the church car park, free. The Kongevegen over Filefjell was forced through this pass in the 1790s at gradients near one in four, which no loaded cart could manage. In the 1840s the section was rebuilt on high dry-stone walls with four hairpin bends, easing it to about one in five. Still too steep: it was abandoned in 1872 after barely thirty years. That early failure is why it survives — nothing modern was ever laid over it, and the 1790s road is still visible running between the bends of the 1840s one.
  • The drive itself: Valdres farmland, then bare high country, then the long descent to the Sognefjord. Stop when it looks good — that is the point of leaving early.

If Time Allows

  • Feigefossen: one of Norway’s tallest free-falling waterfalls, up the fjord at Feigum in Luster, a steepish 30–40 minute walk from the road.
  • Kaupanger Stave Church, twelve minutes from the cabin: its timber was felled in 1137, it has more staves than any other — 22 in the nave plus four free-standing — and it is the third church on that spot, with two burnt predecessors found under the floor. Tours run 10:00–15:00 through 20 September — one for a Wednesday that runs early.

Nigardsbreen

Crampons on an arm of Europe’s largest mainland glacier. Then six hours back to Oslo.

Glacier: cold, wet, windy — dress in layers Waterproofs and boots required
Ellis + Elliot only
Ice hike — private, NigardsbreenComfort Hotel, Oslo

Logistics

  • Thu ~8:00 AM — out of the Sogndal cabin, about 1.5 hours up Jostedalen.
  • A boat across the meltwater lake and a walk-in separate the car park from the ice. Parking coordinates are on the map chip above.
  • 09:45–14:15 — on the glacier. Private guide, crampons and harness supplied.
  • ~2:30 PM — off the ice and back to the car, then roughly 6 hours to Oslo.

Must-Do

  • The blue ice, just the two of them. Ellis, Elliot and a private guide. Nigardsbreen is an arm of Jostedalsbreen, the largest glacier on mainland Europe: crampons biting, walking between crevasses on ice that is compressed snow hundreds of years old, at whatever pace the two of them set. This is the hard thing on the trip, and the one Ellis will still be talking about.
  • The approach: the tongue sits at the head of a meltwater lake in a bare rock valley, and there are marker stones on the walk in showing where the ice reached in past centuries. It has pulled back a very long way.

If Time Allows

  • Breheimsenteret at the valley mouth — glacier exhibits, a warm room and hot food on the way out.
  • The drive back retraces the E16 past Borgund, so if Wednesday ran late, Vindhellavegen is still there on the way home.
